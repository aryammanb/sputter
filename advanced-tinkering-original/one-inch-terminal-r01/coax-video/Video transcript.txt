0:00 The compact coax connection
This is the proposed compact coax connection for our magnetron. The cable on the right comes from the matching box. It plugs into a bulkhead S M A socket on the outside end of the service tube. A second coax cable continues inside that tube. The two coolant hoses use separate passages below it. The terminal plate is actual C A D geometry; the connector internals shown here are explanatory geometry, not a manufacturer's detailed model.

0:28 How the external plug mates
Watch the external plug approach the fixed socket. The male center pin enters the female center contact. The outer metal contacts meet around the insulating region. The coupling nut then threads onto the socket body and retains the connection. We turn the coupling nut rather than twisting the cable. This animation separates alignment and tightening for clarity; the contact and thread details are simplified.

0:53 One continuous RF center path
The orange parts show the live center path. The external cable center connects to the male pin, then to the female contact, and onward to the internal coax center conductor. These are intentionally connected. There is no voltage isolation between the external coax center and the internal coax center. They form one R F electrical node. The isolation we need is between that center path and the surrounding grounded metal.

1:20 What provides the insulation
The cream colored material is the dielectric. Inside the S M A connector, an insulating support such as P T F E separates the center contact from the outer shell. Inside each coax cable, its dielectric separates the center conductor from the surrounding shield. The outer cable jacket is an additional protective layer. The metal retaining nut and washer are mounting hardware, not electrical insulators. The socket shell is intentionally bonded to the metal terminal plate.

1:50 How the internal cable attaches
At the rear of the bulkhead socket, the internal cable is terminated as a coax assembly. Its center conductor connects to the center contact, using the connector manufacturer's specified assembly method. Its shield connects to the connector body through the rear termination and crimp ferrule. A correctly assembled commercial pigtail provides this connection; we do not need a loose bare wire hanging off the back of the S M A socket. The outer jacket ends behind the shield termination.

2:18 The transition at the cathode
Now move to the magnetron head. These sectioned bodies come from the reconstructed source C A D. The copper head and attached spacer are live; the bottom body and service tube are grounded. The main P T F E ring separates them. The proposed coax shield bond is on the grounded structure. The center conductor continues with dielectric protection to a separate live terminal. Only the short terminal connection is exposed. The shield must not connect to the live cathode. This head termination is a concept overlay; its clamp, insulation length and clearances still require detailed R F design.

2:55 Keep the two electrical paths distinct
Follow the two paths. The orange center path carries the R F connection from the matching box to the cathode. The blue gray outer path joins the cable shields, connector shells and grounded structure as the intended return. Solid dielectric separates the coax conductors, and the head insulator separates the live mechanical assembly from ground. This is an explanation of connectivity, not a calculated electric field or an electrical rating. We still need to verify the actual connector, cable, bias voltage and ignition conditions against the matching box output.
