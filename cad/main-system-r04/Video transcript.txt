6:06 Why we chose twelve magnet stacks
The selected arrangement uses readily available N42 disc magnets. Each of the twelve outer positions holds two six millimetre diameter, ten millimetre long magnets, giving a twenty millimetre stack. The centre contains two twenty millimetre diameter by ten millimetre magnets, also giving twenty millimetres total length. The outer stacks sit on a fifty eight millimetre pitch circle. The centre presents north toward the target and the outside stacks present south. Compared with the earlier outer-dominant arrangement, the larger centre and smaller outer poles move the design toward a balanced magnetron. Twelve positions give a more evenly distributed ring than the six-position alternative. The outer to centre pole-area proxy is one point zero eight. That is a useful selection aid, not a measured flux ratio or proof of a balanced topology.

6:56 Magnetic circuit and calculated field
The copper and steel pieces were revised around this pack: a twenty one millimetre centre bore, six point four millimetre outer wells, and a seventy one point five millimetre outer diameter. The steel return path guides flux between opposing poles. A separate three dimensional nonlinear magnetostatic study evaluated this candidate using N42 remanence and an assumed AISI ten ten steel curve. At a quarter millimetre above the modeled nonmagnetic target, the calculated parallel field averaged ninety five point one millitesla around a racetrack near twenty millimetres radius. It ranged approximately eighty nine point five to ninety nine point five millitesla. These are field calculations under stated material and spacing assumptions. They do not predict ignition, deposition rate, erosion profile or plasma confinement time, and they do not by themselves certify the balanced topology. Copper changes the spacing; it is not a static magnetic shield.

15:38 How magnetron sputtering works
Here the glow and particles are an illustration of the process, not a simulated discharge. After evacuation and controlled argon admission, electrical power creates and sustains a plasma. Argon ions accelerate through the sheath toward the target and knock target atoms out of its surface. The magnetic field curves electron motion and helps keep electrons near the target, increasing ionization locally and producing the familiar racetrack region. The magnetic field does not simply pull all of the sputtered atoms onto the substrate. Many ejected target atoms are neutral and travel through the gas toward the substrate and chamber surfaces, with possible collisions. The coloured moving dots are explanatory symbols. Their sizes, speeds, numbers and paths are exaggerated and do not represent pressure, deposition rate or a calculated energy distribution.

16:27 Shutter closed: conditioning the target
With the shutter closed, the source can be conditioned before intentionally exposing the substrate. The blade intercepts the direct deposition path to the sample. It does not stop the plasma, seal off the substrate or prevent every scattered atom from reaching it. The illustrated atom paths terminate at the blade in this simplified view. Opening the shutter then exposes the sample to the source. This is a conceptual sequence, not an operating recipe: actual base pressure, argon flow, working pressure, power, warm-up time and conditioning duration need experimental development within the hardware limits. Cooling and pressure permissives must be established before enabling the source.
